Real Voice or AI Voice? Robust Detection of AI-Sung Chinese Songs

Anqi Wang

Leiden University

Code Results Audio demo
Summary Singing-voice deepfake detectors are usually evaluated on clean audio, but do they still work after common edits like pitch shifting or adding reverb? In this project, we tested how well singing-voice deepfake detectors handle edited audio. Using Mandarin vocals from CtrSVDD, I applied pitch shifts, tempo changes, noise and reverb, and compared detectors based on wav2vec2, WavLM and MERT. Every edit reduced performance. WavLM was the most accurate on clean audio, but after a one-semitone pitch shift it labelled nearly all real singers as AI. The same happened when the pitch was shifted up and back down, so the detectors seem to react to the processing itself. Training with some edited clips helped a lot; a more complex classifier did not.

Motivation

Around 10% of the tracks delivered to the streaming platform Deezer are AI-generated [1]. Frohmann et al. [1] detect them from lyrics transcripts, which is robust to audio edits but only catches AI-written lyrics. It has a blind spot: human-written songs sung by an AI voice, such as AI covers.

Singing-voice deepfake detection (SVDD) targets exactly this case. Chinese datasets and strong detectors exist [2, 3, 4], but they are mostly evaluated on clean audio. Hurt & Yang [6] recently tested frozen speech encoders on CtrSVDD under noise, reverberation and lossy coding. They leave music-pretrained encoders and training-time augmentation open. Pitch shift and tempo change, the most common edits in music, have so far been studied only for full-song music detectors [7]. Real AI covers are often pitch-shifted, sped up, or mixed with reverb. Does detection survive those edits?

Research questions

  • RQ1. How much do pitch shift, tempo change, noise and reverb hurt a singing-voice deepfake detector?
  • RQ2. Which edit hurts most, and why? Does it cause missed fakes or false alarms on real singers?
  • RQ3. Do stronger or music-trained front ends make the detector more robust?
  • RQ4. Can training with (different) edits recover the lost performance?

Data

We use the Mandarin part of CtrSVDD [3], the dataset of the controlled track of the SVDD 2024 challenge [4]. Real vocals come from M4Singer and Opencpop. AI vocals are re-synthesised from the same songs with several singing-voice synthesis and conversion systems.

Method

We follow the self-supervised front-end approach of Wang & Yamagishi [5]. A pretrained model describes the audio, and a simple classifier decides real or AI. Only the classifier is trained, and the pretrained model stays frozen.

Front ends compared

  • wav2vec2-base: a small speech model, used as our simple baseline.
  • WavLM-Large: a large speech model of the kind used by top SVDD 2024 systems [4].
  • MERT-95M: a model pretrained on music, to test whether music knowledge helps.

Making the test fair

  • Unseen singers, rotated: the 10 M4Singer singers are split into 5 folds of 2. Each fold is tested once while the other 9 singers train the classifier, and we report mean ± std over the 5 folds. The layer and the decision threshold are chosen on the training singers only.
  • Paired comparison: the same test clips are used for every edit, so a drop can only come from the edit.
  • Different edits in training and testing for the augmentation experiment (e.g. train with ±1 semitone, test with +2 to +6).

Edits

Pitch shift +1, +2, +4, +6 semitones · tempo ×0.8, ×1.2 · white noise at 20 and 10 dB SNR · synthetic room reverb with RT60 0.5 s and 1.0 s.

We report the equal error rate (EER), the standard metric in deepfake detection (lower is better, and 0.5 means guessing). We also report how often real and AI clips are classified correctly.

Control: edit, then undo it

To separate the musical change from the processing, we shift the pitch up and back down again (or speed up and slow down again). The result has the original pitch and tempo, but carries the processing traces twice.

Training with edits (augmentation)

We add the training singers' clips again with edits that are never used in testing (pitch −1 and −3 st, tempo ×0.9 and ×1.1, noise 30 dB, reverb 0.3 s). The last column trains without any edit of the tested kind, e.g. for pitch shift only tempo, noise and reverb edits are added.

Findings

  • Every edit hurts every model. Across all three front ends and all ten edits, the EER rises from 0.07–0.13 on clean audio to 0.20–0.42.
  • Best on clean ≠ most robust. WavLM-Large has the lowest clean EER (0.067 ± 0.023). Trained on clean audio, after any pitch shift, tempo change or reverb it labels about 98–99% of real clips as AI, consistently across the five folds (std ≤ 0.02).
  • The failure direction depends on the front end. WavLM fails with false alarms on real singers. MERT fails the other way: under pitch shift, tempo change and reverb, AI clips pass as real (AI correct falls from 0.87 to 0.23 at +6 semitones). Noise causes false alarms for all three models.
  • The detectors react to processing traces, not to the change. Shifting the pitch up and back down leaves the pitch unchanged, yet hurts as much or more (WavLM: EER 0.350 for +2 st, 0.408 for +2 st and back; tempo ×1.2: 0.345, and back: 0.425).
  • A stronger classifier does not fix it; better training data does. An attentive pooling head trained on clean audio changes almost nothing (WavLM still labels over 99% of edited real clips as AI). Training the simple classifier with a few edited clips roughly halves WavLM's EER under small pitch shifts and tempo changes (+1 st: 0.343 → 0.168, ×1.2: 0.345 → 0.160), and brings real-clip accuracy from 1% back to 79%. The cost on clean audio is small (0.067 → 0.086).
  • Augmentation transfers between edit types, except noise. Without any pitch edit in training, WavLM's EER at +1 st still drops to 0.177. Pitch, tempo and reverb edits help each other; noise is not helped by the other edit types.
  • After augmentation, WavLM and MERT are close. WavLM is best on clean audio, noise and reverb, and MERT on pitch and tempo changes.
  • Shallow-to-middle layers work best. The layers chosen are 1–2 of 12 (wav2vec2), 4–5 of 12 (MERT) and 6–10 of 24 (WavLM), in line with [6], where middle layers beat the last layer.

Limitations

  • The detector is deliberately simple (frozen front end + linear classifier), not a state-of-the-art system.
  • Edits are simulated in code (librosa's phase-vocoder pitch shift and time stretch, synthetic reverb). Higher-quality production tools may leave fewer processing traces, so the size of the effect may differ.
  • With about 1,600 training clips per fold, the attentive head may be under-trained; the conclusion about heads holds at this data scale.
  • CtrSVDD contains isolated vocals. Real songs have accompaniment, which is future work (e.g. WildSVDD [4]).
  • Only a few Mandarin singers are available, so results can vary between test singers.

Reproduce

Download dev.txt, dev_set.zip, train.txt and train_set.zip from Zenodo into data/, then:

pip install -r requirements.txt
python rotate.py                                     # wav2vec2-base, 5 singer folds
python rotate.py --model microsoft/wavlm-large
python rotate.py --model m-a-p/MERT-v1-95M
python attn_head.py --model microsoft/wavlm-large    # attentive head (after rotate.py)
python aug.py --model microsoft/wavlm-large          # augmentation (after rotate.py)
python rotate.py --model microsoft/wavlm-large --suffix _roundtrip \
    --edits clean,pitch:2,pitchrt:2,tempo:1.2,temport:1.2   # round-trip control
python make_site_data.py                             # update this page
