Real Voice or AI Voice? Robust Detection of AI-Sung Chinese Songs

Anqi Wang · MSc Computer Science (AI), Leiden University

Code on GitHub Results Listen
TL;DR Singing-voice deepfake detectors are evaluated on clean audio. We test three of them on Mandarin singing after everyday edits (pitch shift, tempo change, noise, reverb), with every singer taking a turn as an unseen test singer. Every edit hurts. The detector that is best on clean audio (WavLM) is the most fragile: after a one-semitone pitch shift it calls almost every real singer fake. The music-pretrained MERT holds up best under pitch and tempo changes, but fails the other way, letting AI-sung clips pass as real.

Motivation

Around 10% of the tracks delivered to the streaming platform Deezer are AI-generated [1]. Frohmann et al. [1] detect them from lyrics transcripts, which is robust to audio edits but only catches AI-written lyrics. It has a blind spot: human-written songs sung by an AI voice, such as AI covers.

Singing-voice deepfake detection (SVDD) targets exactly this case. Chinese datasets and strong detectors exist [2, 3, 4], but they are mostly evaluated on clean audio. Hurt & Yang [6] recently tested frozen speech encoders on CtrSVDD under noise, reverberation and lossy coding. They leave music-pretrained encoders and training-time augmentation open. Pitch shift and tempo change, the most common edits in music, have so far been studied only for full-song music detectors [7]. Real AI covers are often pitch-shifted, sped up, or mixed with reverb. Does detection survive those edits?

Research questions

  • RQ1. How much do pitch shift, tempo change, noise and reverb hurt a singing-voice deepfake detector?
  • RQ2. Which edit hurts most, and why? Does it cause missed fakes or false alarms on real singers?
  • RQ3. Do stronger or music-trained front ends make the detector more robust?
  • RQ4. Can training with (different) edits recover the lost performance?

Data

We use the Mandarin part of CtrSVDD [3], the dataset of the controlled track of the SVDD 2024 challenge [4]. Real vocals come from M4Singer and Opencpop. AI vocals are re-synthesised from the same songs with several singing-voice synthesis and conversion systems.

We use a balanced subset: 100 real + 100 AI clips per singer (2,200 clips in total), with the AI clips spread evenly over the generation methods.

Method

We follow the self-supervised front-end approach of Wang & Yamagishi [5]. A pretrained model describes the audio, and a simple classifier decides real or AI. Only the classifier is trained, and the pretrained model stays frozen.

Front ends compared

  • wav2vec2-base: a small speech model, used as our simple baseline.
  • WavLM-Large: a large speech model of the kind used by top SVDD 2024 systems [4].
  • MERT-95M: a model pretrained on music, to test whether music knowledge helps.

Making the test fair

  • Unseen singers, rotated: the 10 M4Singer singers are split into 5 folds of 2. Each fold is tested once while the other 9 singers train the classifier, and we report mean ± std over the 5 folds. The layer and the decision threshold are chosen on the training singers only.
  • Paired comparison: the same test clips are used for every edit, so a drop can only come from the edit.
  • Different edits in training and testing for the augmentation experiment (e.g. train with ±1 semitone, test with +2 to +6).

Edits

Pitch shift +1, +2, +4, +6 semitones · tempo ×0.8, ×1.2 · white noise at 20 and 10 dB SNR · synthetic room reverb with RT60 0.5 s and 1.0 s.

Results

We report the equal error rate (EER), the standard metric in deepfake detection (lower is better, and 0.5 means guessing). We also report how often real and AI clips are classified correctly.

Findings so far

  • Every edit hurts every model. Across all three front ends and all ten edits, the EER rises from 0.07–0.13 on clean audio to 0.20–0.42.
  • Best on clean ≠ most robust. WavLM-Large has the lowest clean EER (0.067 ± 0.023). After any pitch shift, tempo change or reverb, it labels about 98–99% of real clips as AI, consistently across the five folds (std ≤ 0.02).
  • The failure direction depends on the front end. WavLM fails with false alarms on real singers. MERT fails the other way: under pitch shift, tempo change and reverb, real clips stay correct but AI clips pass as real (AI correct falls from 0.87 to 0.23 at +6 semitones). Noise causes false alarms for all three models.
  • No model wins everywhere. MERT is most robust to pitch and tempo changes (EER 0.22–0.29 vs. 0.28–0.39), WavLM to noise (0.20 and 0.29), and on reverb no model is clearly better.
  • The differences are larger than the singer-to-singer variation. For example, at +1 semitone, the EER is 0.343 ± 0.022 for WavLM and 0.237 ± 0.016 for MERT. On clean audio, wav2vec2 and MERT cannot be told apart.
  • Shallow-to-middle layers work best. The layers chosen are 1–2 of 12 (wav2vec2), 4–5 of 12 (MERT) and 6–10 of 24 (WavLM), in line with [6], where middle layers beat the last layer.

Next: a stronger, attention-based detection head (does it fix the collapse?) and training with augmentation.

Limitations

  • The detector is deliberately simple (frozen front end + linear classifier), not a state-of-the-art system.
  • Edits are simulated in code (librosa pitch shift and time stretch, synthetic reverb), so they approximate real production tools.
  • CtrSVDD contains isolated vocals. Real songs have accompaniment, which is future work (e.g. WildSVDD [4]).
  • Only a few Mandarin singers are available, so results can vary between test singers.
